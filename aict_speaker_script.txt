Offline Multimodal Edge AI Framework for ICT Accessibility Systems
================================================================

SLIDE 1: TITLE SLIDE
-------------------
"Good morning/afternoon everyone. Thank you for joining this session. My name is Mohammad Al Bataineh, and I'm an Associate Professor in the Electrical and Communication Engineering Department at United Arab Emirates University.

Today, I'm excited to present our work on an Offline Multimodal Edge AI Framework that integrates computer vision and speech processing for ICT accessibility systems.

This is a collaborative effort with my colleagues Fitsum Mekonnen, Ahmed Serag, Kena Teshale, Dana Abu Abdoun, Winner Abula, and Simon Darota from the Mechanical Engineering, Electrical Engineering, and Computer Science departments at UAEU.

Our work addresses critical challenges in privacy-preserving assistive technologies for individuals with visual impairments."

[PAUSE - Take a breath, make eye contact]


SLIDE 2: MOTIVATION & PROBLEM STATEMENT
---------------------------------------
"Let me start by explaining the motivation behind this work.

Current assistive systems that help visually impaired individuals typically rely on cloud-based processing. While these systems can be powerful, they introduce several critical limitations:

First, PRIVACY CONCERNS. Users must transmit sensitive personal data - including video of their surroundings, audio recordings, and biometric information - to external servers. For vulnerable populations, this raises serious privacy and security concerns.

Second, NETWORK DEPENDENCY. These systems require constant internet connectivity. In areas with poor infrastructure or during network outages, the system becomes completely non-functional.

Third, LATENCY ISSUES. Network delays can significantly impact the user experience, especially when real-time feedback is critical for navigation or interaction.

[PAUSE]

Our solution takes a fundamentally different approach. We've developed a completely OFFLINE, privacy-first system that runs entirely on edge hardware - specifically, a Raspberry Pi 5.

Our framework provides:
- 100% offline operation with complete data sovereignty
- Integration of multiple AI modalities - vision, speech, and text processing
- Real-time performance with sub-1.2 second latency per modality
- All of this on affordable, resource-efficient hardware

This is about giving users complete control over their data while maintaining the functionality they need."


SLIDE 3: KEY CONTRIBUTIONS
--------------------------
"Now let me highlight our key contributions and the performance we've achieved.

Looking at these metrics, you can see the impact of our optimization strategies:

We achieved a 23 to 31 percent reduction in inference time through dynamic optimization techniques. This brings object detection from 1100 milliseconds down to 850 milliseconds.

Our intelligent caching mechanism reduced redundant OCR processing by 45 percent, significantly improving overall system throughput.

On the privacy front, our system scores 9.8 out of 10 on our composite privacy metric - we'll discuss this methodology later. This is because we achieve ZERO external data transmission.

Perhaps most importantly for real-world deployment, we achieved ZERO system crashes during 50 hours of continuous testing. This demonstrates the robustness of our approach.

[PAUSE]

Our novel contributions fall into four categories:

First, OPTIMIZATION STRATEGIES. We developed queue-based inference management, adaptive buffer control, priority-based scheduling, and intelligent caching mechanisms specifically designed for resource-constrained hardware.

Second, INTEGRATED ARCHITECTURE. This is the first demonstration of concurrent execution of YOLOv8 object detection, Tesseract OCR, face recognition, AND VOSK speech processing - all running simultaneously on a Raspberry Pi 5.

Third, PRIVACY-FIRST DESIGN. We eliminate all external data transmission while maintaining full functionality. This addresses one of the most critical concerns in assistive technology deployment.

Fourth, APPLICATION-SPECIFIC CHARACTERIZATION. We systematically evaluated performance across three real-world scenarios: environmental awareness, text reading, and social interaction - each with defined latency thresholds and measured performance."

--------------------------------
"To put our work in context, let me show you how it compares to existing multimodal edge-based assistive systems.

This table summarizes five different approaches:

Dubey and colleagues in 2019 demonstrated vision-only systems on Raspberry Pi with high privacy but limited to a single function.

Mahendran's work in 2021 added navigation capabilities but used a cloud-hybrid approach, compromising privacy for performance.

Patil and Pawar in 2024 combined vision and audio but lacked OCR and face recognition capabilities.

Okolo's recent work focused specifically on navigation with good latency but limited scope.

[PAUSE - Point to the highlighted row]

Our framework is shown in the bottom row. We integrate FOUR OR MORE modalities - object detection, OCR, face recognition, speech recognition, and text-to-speech - while maintaining FULL privacy with a score of 9.8 out of 10.

Our latency ranges from 850 to 1240 milliseconds for individual modalities. The key limitation is that we target stationary or slow-moving scenarios rather than high-speed navigation.

[TRANSITION]

This comparison reveals three critical research gaps that our work addresses:

First, LIMITED MULTIMODAL INTEGRATION. Previous systems focus on only one or two modalities, lacking comprehensive environmental understanding.

Second, INSUFFICIENT OPTIMIZATION. Prior work doesn't systematically address the challenges of concurrent execution on embedded platforms.

Third, PRIVACY-PERFORMANCE TRADE-OFFS. Cloud-hybrid approaches compromise privacy for performance.

Our framework addresses all three gaps through novel optimization strategies that enable full offline multimodal operation."


SLIDE 5: SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE
----------------------------
"Let me walk you through our system architecture.

[Point to the diagram]

The framework is built around a modular architecture with three input streams at the top:
- Camera input for visual processing
- Microphone input for audio analysis  
- User commands for direct system control

These inputs feed into three specialized processing modules:
- The Computer Vision Module uses YOLOv8 for object detection
- The Speech Processing Module handles voice recognition with VOSK
- The Natural Language Processing Module interprets text and commands

At the heart of the system is the Central Processing Coordinator. This is critical - it manages task scheduling, resource allocation, and communication between modules. The coordinator implements our priority-based scheduling system to ensure responsive interaction even under high load.

Finally, the coordinator generates two types of output:
- Audio Feedback that announces detected objects, recognized faces, or read text to the user
- System Response for control and status updates

[PAUSE]

The key innovation here is the modular design. Each processing module can operate independently and be updated without affecting system stability. But the coordinator ensures they work together efficiently, sharing limited computational resources intelligently.

This architecture enables what we call 'adaptive interaction' - the system responds intelligently to evolving user needs by prioritizing the most important tasks in real-time."


SLIDE 6: HARDWARE PLATFORM & SOFTWARE STACK
-------------------------------------------
"Now let's discuss the hardware and software implementation.

On the hardware side, we chose the Raspberry Pi 5 as our core processing platform. It has an ARM Cortex-A76 processor running at 2.4 gigahertz with 8 gigabytes of LPDDR4X RAM.

Why Raspberry Pi? Three reasons: computational capability, power efficiency, and cost-effectiveness. The entire hardware configuration costs less than 200 dollars.

We use a Camera Module V3 with a 12-megapixel sensor capturing 1080p video at 30 frames per second for visual input.

For audio, we have a USB microphone array with 3-element beamforming for better voice capture in noisy environments.

[TRANSITION to software]

On the software side, we carefully selected models that balance accuracy and computational efficiency:

For object detection, we use YOLOv8 nano - the smallest, fastest variant of YOLO v8.

For text recognition, we use Tesseract 5.x, which is the state-of-the-art open-source OCR engine.

Face recognition is handled by OpenCV's DNN module with pre-trained models.

For speech-to-text, we use VOSK - critically, VOSK is designed for OFFLINE operation, which is essential for our privacy-first approach.

Finally, we use pyttsx3 for text-to-speech audio feedback.

[PAUSE]

The entire software stack implements a multi-threaded architecture. This is essential - we maximize utilization of the available CPU cores while maintaining responsive user interaction. The user never experiences the system as 'frozen' even when processing multiple tasks simultaneously.

This combination of carefully selected hardware and optimized software is what makes privacy-preserving multimodal AI practical on consumer-grade edge devices."


SLIDE 7: OPTIMIZATION STRATEGIES
---------------------------------
"The key to making this system work on resource-constrained hardware is our four-pillar optimization approach. This is really the heart of our technical contribution.

[Point to diagram]

This workflow diagram shows how data flows through the system with our optimizations applied.

Let me explain each optimization:

[Point to first card]

FIRST: Queue-Based Inference Optimization. We implement a prioritized inference queue that prevents system crashes during high CPU or memory usage. We dynamically manage task allocation and limit the number of objects detected per frame to maintain stability.

The result? We reduced inference delay from approximately 1100 milliseconds to 850 milliseconds - that's a 23 percent reduction - while maintaining over 80 percent detection accuracy.

[Point to second card]

SECOND: Adaptive Buffer Management. We use circular queues with dynamic size adjustment. The buffer capacity scales from 10 to 50 commands depending on current system load. When latency exceeds 1000 milliseconds, the buffer automatically expands.

This reduced memory crashes by approximately 90 percent compared to fixed-size buffers. In our 50-hour test, we had ZERO crashes.

[Point to third card]

THIRD: Priority-Based Task Scheduling. This is crucial for maintaining responsive interaction. We implement real-time prioritization:
- Audio processing gets Priority 1 with a target under 500 milliseconds
- Face recognition is Priority 2, under 1000 milliseconds  
- Object detection is Priority 3, also under 1000 milliseconds
- OCR is Priority 4 with a target under 1500 milliseconds

Under high load, this scheduling reduced voice-command response time by 60 percent - from 1.5 seconds down to 0.6 seconds. Even when the system is processing video and detecting objects, voice commands still get immediate attention.

[Point to fourth card]

FOURTH: Intelligent Caching. When we detect identical or highly similar text regions - more than 95 percent similarity - we reuse previously processed results instead of re-running OCR.

This reduced redundant text processing by approximately 45 percent, particularly helpful when users repeatedly look at the same document or sign.

[PAUSE]

Together, these four optimizations enable effective multimodal AI execution on hardware that costs less than 200 dollars."


SLIDE 8: EXPERIMENTAL METHODOLOGY
---------------------------------
"Let me describe our rigorous testing methodology.

We wanted to ensure our system works in real-world conditions, not just ideal laboratory settings.

[Left column]

For ENVIRONMENTAL CONDITIONS, we tested across three lighting scenarios:
- Well-lit at 400 to 500 lux, simulating office environments
- Moderate lighting at 100 to 200 lux, typical indoor conditions  
- Dim lighting at 30 to 50 lux for low-light situations

For audio, we tested in quiet conditions with less than 40 decibels ambient noise, and noisy conditions at 60 to 70 decibels.

[Right column]

Our PERFORMANCE METRICS were comprehensive:

Processing latency was measured end-to-end - from the moment input is acquired until audio feedback is generated to the user.

For accuracy, we calculated both precision and recall using standard formulas.

We monitored resource utilization at 100-millisecond intervals to understand CPU and memory patterns.

[PAUSE]

Critically, all measurements were repeated 50 times per condition. We report results as mean plus or minus standard deviation. Statistical validation was performed using paired t-tests with p-value less than 0.05.

[BOTTOM SECTION]

We evaluated three APPLICATION SCENARIOS that represent real use cases:

Scenario 1 - ENVIRONMENTAL AWARENESS: Object detection for obstacle identification and spatial understanding. Target latency: less than 1000 milliseconds.

Scenario 2 - TEXT READING: OCR for documents, signage, and labels. Target latency: less than 1500 milliseconds, because this is a user-initiated, non-critical task.

Scenario 3 - SOCIAL INTERACTION: Face recognition for identifying known individuals during social encounters. Target latency: less than 1000 milliseconds.

These scenarios and targets are based on consultation with assistive technology literature and represent practical, real-world usage patterns."

However, our priority scheduling ensures voice commands maintain less than 700 milliseconds response even during concurrent operations.

The statistical validation is important. Using paired t-tests with p-value less than 0.05, we confirmed:

Our optimizations reduce latency by 28 percent compared to baseline implementation - this is statistically significant with p equals 0.003.

Most importantly for real-world deployment: ZERO system crashes in 50 hours of continuous testing.

These results demonstrate that privacy-preserving multimodal AI is not just theoretically possible - it's practically viable for real-world assistive technology applications."

The Vision Module runs at 45.2 percent - this is our highest individual module usage, which makes sense given the complexity of object detection.

[RIGHT COLUMN - Memory]

Memory management is equally important:

Baseline usage is stable at 1.2 gigabytes, plus or minus 0.2 gigabytes.

Even at peak load, memory remains stable at 1.4 gigabytes.

Our adaptive buffers scale capacity from 10 to 50 commands as needed.

The result? Zero memory overflow events in 50 hours of testing. Compare this to 15 crashes we observed with fixed-size buffers in preliminary testing.

[MIDDLE SECTION - Quantization]

Our dynamic quantization strategy demonstrates intelligent performance-accuracy trade-offs:

Under LOW LOAD - less than 50 percent CPU - we use FP16 precision and achieve 85.3 percent accuracy. We have computational headroom, so we maximize accuracy.

Under HIGH LOAD - more than 80 percent CPU - the system automatically switches to INT8 quantization. Accuracy drops slightly to 81.2 percent, but we maintain 850-millisecond latency.

This is a 4 percent accuracy trade-off that prevents system unresponsiveness. The user gets slightly less accurate results, but the system remains responsive. This is the right trade-off for real-world usage.

[CONCLUSION]

The key achievement here is effective multimodal AI execution on resource-constrained hardware through priority-based scheduling and adaptive resource allocation. The system intelligently manages limited resources to maintain responsiveness while maximizing overall performance."


SLIDE 11: PRIVACY-PERFORMANCE TRADE-OFF ANALYSIS
------------------------------------------------
"One of the most important aspects of our work is demonstrating that you don't have to sacrifice privacy for performance.

Let me explain our privacy scoring methodology.

[POINT TO FORMULA]

We define a composite privacy metric with three components:

L-sub-p measures LOCAL PROCESSING. This gets a score of 10 if all processing is local, 0 if cloud-dependent.

N-sub-t measures NO EXTERNAL TRANSMISSION. This scores 10 if there's zero data transmission, 0 if data is sent externally.

U-sub-c measures USER CONTROL over their data, on a scale of 0 to 10.

The final privacy score is a weighted sum: 0.4 times local processing, plus 0.4 times no transmission, plus 0.2 times user control. We weight data locality and transmission privacy most heavily because these are the primary privacy concerns.

[TRANSITION TO TABLE]

Now let's see how different systems score:

OUR EDGE SYSTEM scores:
- L-p equals 10: All processing is completely local
- N-t equals 10: Zero external data transmission  
- U-c equals 9: Full user control with only minor OS dependencies
- Overall score: 9.8 out of 10

OTHER EDGE IMPLEMENTATIONS typically score 7.2 to 8.6 out of 10. Many still have some cloud connectivity or data transmission.

CLOUD-BASED SYSTEMS score only 2 to 4 out of 10. Even though they may have high accuracy, they fundamentally compromise privacy by transmitting data externally. N-t equals zero because of external transmission.

[PAUSE - emphasize]

Look at the response times. Our system achieves 850 to 1680 milliseconds - competitive with other edge implementations and often FASTER than cloud systems when you account for network latency.

The key insight is this: Superior privacy - 9.8 out of 10 - with competitive performance. No trade-offs. Privacy without sacrificing functionality.

This is what makes our approach suitable for real-world deployment where users care deeply about data sovereignty."

SCENARIO 1 - ENVIRONMENTAL AWARENESS:

Target latency is under 1000 milliseconds. We achieved 980 milliseconds, plus or minus 150.

[BOTTOM SECTION - very important]

Now, it's critical to discuss what our system is suitable for and what it's NOT designed for:

[LEFT - SUITABLE FOR]

Our system works well for:
- Stationary or slow-moving users
- Environmental scanning and awareness
- Document and label reading  
- Social interaction assistance
- Indoor navigation at walking pace

[RIGHT - LIMITATIONS]

Current limitations:

High-speed navigation requiring sub-500 millisecond latencies. If someone is running or moving quickly, they need faster response than we currently provide.

Rapid obstacle avoidance during running - same issue.

Extreme low-light conditions below 30 lux - performance degrades.

Very high ambient noise above 70 decibels - speech recognition accuracy drops.

[BOTTOM NOTE - read this carefully]

This is important: High-speed navigation typically requires sub-500 millisecond latencies. Our system achieves application-appropriate latencies for stationary and slow-moving scenarios.

However, we expect hardware acceleration - such as adding a Coral TPU or Raspberry Pi AI Kit - to reduce our latencies to 200 to 400 milliseconds, which WOULD enable high-speed navigation applications.

So this is not a fundamental limitation of our approach, but rather a hardware limitation that can be addressed in future iterations."


SLIDE 13: CONCLUSIONS & FUTURE DIRECTIONS
-----------------------------------------
"Let me summarize our key achievements and discuss future directions.

[TOP SECTION - Achievements]

We've demonstrated five key achievements:

First, PRIVACY-FIRST DESIGN: Complete offline operation with a 9.8 out of 10 privacy score. No user data ever leaves the device.

Second, MULTIMODAL INTEGRATION: This is the first demonstration of 4-plus AI modalities running concurrently on Raspberry Pi 5 - object detection, OCR, face recognition, speech recognition, and text-to-speech.

Third, NOVEL OPTIMIZATIONS: We achieved 23 to 31 percent latency reduction through intelligent resource management - queue-based inference, adaptive buffering, priority scheduling, and intelligent caching.

Fourth, PRACTICAL VIABILITY: The entire system costs less than 200 dollars in hardware, enabling widespread deployment. This is not just a research prototype.

Fifth, ROBUST PERFORMANCE: Zero crashes in 50 hours of continuous testing. This demonstrates production-level reliability.

[MIDDLE SECTION - Limitations]

We acknowledge three current limitations:

Navigation constraint: As discussed, we target stationary and slow-moving scenarios.

Environmental sensitivity: Performance degrades in extreme conditions - very low light or very high noise.

Language support: Currently limited to English voice commands and text recognition.

[BOTTOM SECTION - Future Work]

Our future research will address these limitations through four directions:

First, HARDWARE ACCELERATION: Integration of Coral TPU or Raspberry Pi AI Kit. We expect 3 to 5 times speedup, bringing latencies down to 200 to 400 milliseconds. This would enable high-speed navigation applications.

Second, ENHANCED PREPROCESSING: Developing more robust algorithms for challenging environmental conditions. Better performance in low light and high noise.

Third, MULTI-LANGUAGE SUPPORT: Expanding accessibility to non-English users. The architecture supports this - we just need to integrate additional language models.

Fourth, FIELD EVALUATION: Real-world testing with visually impaired user communities. This is critical to validate effectiveness beyond technical performance metrics and identify deployment barriers.

I should mention that a comprehensive treatment of this research, including extended analysis and additional results, is available in our journal publication in Sensors, which I'll show you in the final slide."
